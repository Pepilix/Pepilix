01 — The Basics

What is a Certificate of Analysis?

A Certificate of Analysis — usually shortened to COA — is a laboratory document reporting the analytical testing carried out on one specific production batch of a compound. It exists to answer two questions in a verifiable way: is this the compound it claims to be, and how much of what's in the vial is genuinely that compound.

A COA is not a marketing claim and it isn't a general statement about a product line. It belongs to a batch — a defined manufacturing run, usually identified by a batch or lot number — and every batch is tested on its own terms. This distinction matters more than it might first appear. A supplier can honestly state that "Retatrutide is HPLC-tested to ≥99% purity" as a general policy, while a specific vial sitting on your bench was drawn from one particular batch, made on one particular date, tested by one particular laboratory. The COA is the document that connects that specific vial back to its own, real testing data — not to the policy, but to the batch.

This is why reputable suppliers issue a new COA for every batch rather than reusing one document across a product's entire sales history. If a peptide has been in a supplier's catalogue for two years, there should be a corresponding sequence of COAs — one per manufacturing run — not a single certificate dated from the first batch ever produced.

The practice itself is borrowed directly from pharmaceutical and industrial chemical manufacturing, where a COA (sometimes called a Certificate of Conformance in adjacent industries) has long been the standard way a manufacturer demonstrates that a specific shipment meets its stated specification before a buyer accepts it. Research peptides sit outside pharmaceutical regulation, which means there's no external body mandating that a COA exists at all, let alone auditing whether it's accurate. That makes the document more important in this space, not less — it's doing work that would otherwise be handled by a regulatory inspection regime, purely on the strength of the testing laboratory's own credibility and the supplier's willingness to publish what it says.

A concrete example makes the batch-versus-product distinction easier to hold onto. Imagine a supplier lists "BPC-157, 10mg" on their site. Over eighteen months of trading, they might work through four separate manufacturing runs of that same product — each with its own batch number, its own manufacture date, and its own COA. A customer who orders in month three receives stock from whichever batch is currently being fulfilled from, and the COA they're entitled to see is the one for that batch specifically, not a generic document describing what the product is supposed to be like in principle.

02 — Why It Matters

Why COAs Matter

For research-grade compounds, which aren't regulated the way pharmaceuticals are, the COA is effectively the only objective link between the label on the vial and what's actually inside it. There's no external inspector checking a supplier's claims before a customer ever sees them — the document itself, and whether it can be independently verified, is doing that job. Five things depend on it directly:

🧬

Identity

Confirms the compound is genuinely the peptide stated on the label, not a related sequence, a degraded fragment, or something else entirely.

⚗️

Purity

Quantifies how much of the detected material corresponds to the target peptide versus everything else that co-exists in the sample.

🔗

Traceability

Ties a specific vial back to a specific manufacturing run, so any quality question can be investigated against real batch data.

🛡️

Quality Assurance

Gives an independent, documented basis for a purity or identity claim, rather than relying on a supplier's word alone.

🔬

Research Confidence

Lets you know what variable you're actually testing against — an uncharacterised sample makes any downstream result harder to interpret or reproduce.

That last point connects to a wider problem in experimental science: a result is only as reproducible as the material used to generate it. If the compound in your vial isn't what the label says — or is contaminated with material at a level nobody disclosed — any downstream observation inherits that uncertainty, whether or not it's ever flagged as a limitation. A COA doesn't solve every reproducibility problem in a research protocol, but it removes one variable that's entirely preventable: not knowing what you were actually working with in the first place.

04 — Field by Field

Breaking Down Every Part of a COA

The same nine fields, laid out for reference — useful if you'd rather read through them directly than click through the example above.

Product Name

The specific compound tested. Should match the label on the vial exactly, including salt form or variant where relevant.

Batch Number

The identifier for this specific manufacturing run. This is what you'd quote if ever contacting the lab or supplier about this exact vial.

Manufacture Date

When the batch was produced. Establishes the starting point for any stability or shelf-life assessment.

Test Date

When the analytical testing was actually performed — should sit close to the manufacture date, not months after.

Expiry

The point up to which the manufacturer's stability data supports the stated purity, assuming correct storage throughout.

Analytical Method

The testing technique used — typically reverse-phase HPLC for purity, often paired with mass spectrometry for identity.

Purity

The peak-area percentage of the target compound relative to everything else detected in the sample.

Specification

The threshold the batch is tested against — for example ≥99% — which the result is then measured up to.

Pass / Fail

Whether the batch met its stated specification. A batch that fails should never reach a customer.

05 — The Biggest Misunderstanding

Purity Is Not Quantity

This is the single most common misreading of a COA: a purity figure of 99.63% does not mean your 10mg vial contains 9.963mg of peptide. It's a much narrower, more specific claim than that — and the difference matters for how you interpret your own results.

What "99.63% Purity" Means
99.63%

Of the material that eluted from the HPLC column and was detected by the instrument, 99.63% of that peak area corresponds to the target peptide. The remainder is other detected material — typically trace synthesis by-products.

What It Does NOT Confirm
? mg

The exact net peptide mass inside the vial. Residual salts, counter-ions, and moisture in the lyophilised cake mean the true peptide content by weight is a separate figure — a "net peptide content" assay — not something the purity percentage tells you on its own.

In short: HPLC purity is a relative measure among what was detected on the column — not an absolute statement of how many milligrams of pure peptide sit in the vial. A complete quality picture uses purity and a separate net content or assay figure where precise dosing matters for your protocol.

A worked example makes this concrete. Suppose a vial is labelled as containing 10mg of a lyophilised peptide, and its COA states 99.63% purity by HPLC. What that figure tells you is that, of the material detected during the chromatography run, 99.63% of the peak area corresponds to the intended peptide. It does not, by itself, tell you that the vial contains 9.963mg of peptide by weight. The lyophilised powder in the vial can include residual acetate or trifluoroacetate counter-ions (common by-products of peptide synthesis), trace moisture, and other non-peptide material that never shows up in an HPLC purity calculation at all, because HPLC purity is calculated only from what the detector sees eluting from the column — not from a separate weighing of the whole sample.

The test that does answer "how much peptide, by mass, is actually in here" is usually called net peptide content, and it's a distinct analytical process — commonly amino acid analysis or a comparable quantitative assay — that most suppliers don't run as standard, because it adds meaningful cost for a figure that many research applications don't strictly require. For work where the exact mass of peptide matters — precise dose-response curves, for instance — it's worth asking a supplier directly whether net content data is available, rather than assuming a high purity percentage answers that question on its own.

06 — The Method Behind the Number

How HPLC Works

High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) is the standard method for determining peptide purity. Understanding roughly how it works makes the purity figure far easier to interpret correctly.

A sample is carried by a solvent through a column packed with material that interacts differently with different molecules. Compounds that interact more weakly with the column pass through faster; compounds that interact more strongly take longer. As each compound exits the column, a detector registers it — producing a peak on a chart plotted against time.

Main peak — the target peptide
Minor peaks — trace impurities, by-products

Purity is calculated from the area under each peak, not its height. The area of the main peak is divided by the total area of every peak detected in the run, then expressed as a percentage. A small, sharp minor peak and a wide, shallow one can represent similar amounts of material — which is exactly why the underlying chromatogram matters more than the purity number in isolation. A number alone is a claim; the chromatogram is the evidence behind it.

Mass Spectrometry: Confirming What You're Actually Looking At

HPLC answers "how clean is this sample" — but it can't answer "is this definitely the right peptide." A synthesis error that produces a slightly wrong sequence, or a truncated fragment missing a few residues, can still elute as a single, sharp, apparently pure peak. HPLC alone would report that peak as high purity, because purity in this context only measures homogeneity — how much of the detected material is one single thing — not whether that one thing is correct.

Mass spectrometry closes that gap. It measures the molecular weight of the compound in the main peak and compares it against the theoretical mass calculated from the peptide's known amino acid sequence. Every peptide has an exact, calculable mass; a correctly synthesised sample should match that calculated value to within a very small tolerance, typically under 1 Dalton for the smaller peptides sold as research compounds. If the observed mass doesn't match — even if HPLC purity looked excellent — that's a genuine identity failure, not a purity issue, and the two tests are answering fundamentally different questions. A complete COA reports both: an HPLC purity result, and an MS-confirmed identity, with both the theoretical and observed mass stated so the comparison is checkable rather than just asserted.

08 — What to Watch For

Red Flags on a COA

Not every document calling itself a COA reflects genuine, batch-specific testing. These are the signs worth checking for before trusting one.

⚠

Missing batch number

Without one, there's no way to tie the document to the actual vial in front of you.

⚠

No test date

Makes it impossible to judge whether testing happened near production, or was never dated at all.

⚠

No analytical method stated

A purity figure with no named method (HPLC, MS, or otherwise) can't be evaluated or reproduced.

⚠

Edited PDFs

Inconsistent fonts, misaligned tables, or visible editing artefacts suggest the document has been altered after the fact.

⚠

Generic COAs

The same certificate reused across multiple batches or listed as covering a whole product line rather than one run.

⚠

Impossible purity claims

Figures that sit implausibly high for the compound class, with nothing to substantiate them.

⚠

Missing chromatogram

A percentage with no underlying chromatogram is a claim you can't independently check.

⚠

No stated specification

Without a threshold to compare against, a "pass" result has nothing to actually be passing.

None of these signs, on their own, prove a COA is fabricated — a genuinely legitimate document can occasionally be missing a minor detail through simple oversight. But taken together, they're a reasonable checklist, and the strongest single verification step is a direct one: contact the testing laboratory named on the certificate yourself, using contact details you find independently rather than ones printed on the document, and confirm the batch and results actually correspond to their records. A COA that can't survive that check isn't providing the traceability it claims to.

10 — Summary

Key Takeaways

Reading a COA properly changes what you can trust

A Certificate of Analysis is only useful if you know what each field actually claims — and just as importantly, what it doesn't.

  • A COA belongs to a batch, not a product line — expect a new one per manufacturing run
  • Purity (HPLC) and identity (MS) are two different tests answering two different questions
  • A purity percentage is a peak-area figure, not a direct statement of net peptide mass
  • The chromatogram is the evidence; the percentage alone is a claim
  • Missing batch numbers, dates, or methods are the clearest signs to look for
